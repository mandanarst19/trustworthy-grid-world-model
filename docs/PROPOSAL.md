# When the Physics Must Be Right: Trustworthy World Models for Power Grid Simulation Under Distribution Shift and Perturbation

## Research Context and Motivation

The concept of a world model — a learned model that simulates the dynamics of a physical environment well enough to support planning, control, and decision-making — has become central to AI research over the past decade. An ordinary world model only needs to predict the next state from the current one. A world model for a power grid must do something harder: it must simulate the consequences of an intervention — what happens to the voltage at every bus if a given line is disconnected, or if a battery is discharged at twice its planned rate — and it must do so under physical constraints that cannot be violated. A world model that hallucinates physically impossible grid states is worse than no model at all: it can generate control actions that damage equipment, destabilize the grid, or create safety hazards that a human operator would have avoided. The trustworthiness of a world model for power grids is therefore not a desirable property; it is a prerequisite for deployment. This proposal investigates how physical law — specifically the AC power flow equations and their associated operational constraints — can be embedded into the architecture and training process of a heterogeneous graph neural network (GNN), so that the resulting world model is trustworthy by construction rather than by empirical validation alone.

My own research provides an unexpected entry point into this problem. In my MSc thesis, I investigated multi-task learning on a physical reservoir — a network of VO₂ thermal neuristors — and found that the material's phase-transition dynamics enforced, without any engineering intervention, the exact conditions that make a multi-task system robust to perturbation: orthogonal task representations, independent output pathways, and differential vulnerability that is predictable from the system's structural properties before any perturbation is applied. The critical insight was that robustness was not a property of the training procedure; it was a property of the physical laws governing the substrate. This raises a question directly relevant to power grid world models: if physical laws are the source of robustness in a material system, can embedding those laws into a learned model produce the same robustness in a system where the physics is not the substrate but the constraint? This proposal is an attempt to answer that question systematically, in the domain of power grid simulation.

## Problem Statement

A GNN trained on power flow data learns, to some degree, to respect physical constraints — not because its architecture encodes these constraints, but simply because the training data respected them. The model's constraint-respecting behavior is therefore implicit rather than explicit, and this implicit learning breaks down in two ways. First, it breaks down under distribution shift: when the grid operates outside the conditions represented in the training data, the model has no mechanism for enforcing constraints it was never forced to explicitly represent. Second, it breaks down under adversarial or fault conditions: an operator action or a component failure that drives the grid into an unusual state produces inputs for which the model's implicit constraint-respecting behavior has never been validated. The solution is to make constraint enforcement explicit — to build it into the architecture, the loss function, and the evaluation protocol — so that trustworthiness becomes a structural property of the model rather than an empirical observation about its training performance. This proposal investigates three complementary approaches to making constraint enforcement explicit: architectural priors that encode physical laws as inductive biases, physics-informed loss formulations that penalize constraint violations during training, and evaluation protocols that measure trustworthiness specifically in the scenarios where implicit constraint learning fails.

Power grid world models also face a specific version of the multi-task robustness problem that my thesis investigated in a physical system. A complete world model for a distribution grid must simultaneously predict bus voltages across all phases, active and reactive power flows at every node, battery state-of-charge trajectories, and control actions for distributed energy resources — all coupled by the grid's physical laws, and all of which must be jointly feasible. A voltage prediction that ignores the constraint imposed by the active power injection at the same bus is not merely inaccurate; it is physically inconsistent. The question of how to learn a joint representation that enforces cross-task consistency — not just empirically, but structurally — is the same question I investigated in my thesis, where cross-task consistency was enforced by the material's phase-transition physics. Here, it must be enforced by the model's architecture and training procedure. Understanding which architectural choices correspond to the physical orthogonality I observed in VO₂ dynamics is one of the central questions this research will address.

## Research Questions

This research is organized around four questions, ordered from foundational to applied.

1. **Structural**: What is the minimal set of physical constraints that must be encoded in a world model's architecture — as opposed to its loss function or its training data — to guarantee physical feasibility under distribution shift?
2. **Representational**: How should the heterogeneous graph structure of a three-phase distribution grid be designed so that the topology of the graph encodes the physical coupling between phases, nodes, and components in a way the GNN can exploit during both training and inference?
3. **Robustness**: Under what perturbations — line outages, load spikes, phase imbalances, measurement noise — does constraint satisfaction degrade, and is this degradation predictable from the model's internal representations before the perturbation occurs?
4. **Evaluative**: What benchmark protocol is necessary to measure trustworthiness specifically in the safety-critical scenarios where existing approaches are weakest, and how should this protocol be designed to distinguish genuine robustness from overfitting to the nominal operating distribution?

## Methodology

**Architectural priors.** The first methodological strand investigates how Kirchhoff's current and voltage laws can be encoded as architectural priors in a heterogeneous GNN, rather than as soft penalty terms in the loss function. Kirchhoff's current law — the sum of currents at every node is zero — is a local constraint involving only a node and its immediate neighbors, which is structurally equivalent to a message-passing operation: the aggregated message at a node from its neighbors, combined with the node's own injection, must sum to zero. This suggests a message-passing scheme that aggregates neighbor messages with a sign convention determined by the direction of power flow, updating node state only in directions that preserve the current balance. The design of such a scheme — its relationship to existing heterogeneous message-passing architectures, its expressivity, and its behavior under the unbalanced three-phase conditions that break the symmetry assumptions of homogeneous GNNs — is the first concrete research objective.

**Physics-informed loss.** The second strand extends existing physics-aware loss formulations for battery state-of-charge, C-rate, and voltage-bound constraints to the full set of constraints governing a world model for power grid simulation. This includes enforcing the AC power flow equations as equality constraints rather than bound constraints, investigating how conflicting constraint types (for example, SoC bounds versus voltage limits) should be weighted against one another, and testing whether physics-informed loss improves out-of-distribution constraint satisfaction or merely redistributes the violation from training scenarios to test scenarios.

**Robustness evaluation.** The third strand designs and implements a robustness evaluation protocol targeted at the failure modes of physics-informed world models under safety-critical perturbations. Existing benchmarks, including SafePowerGraph, evaluate model accuracy under load perturbations of ±10% and ±50% and under topology changes corresponding to N-1 contingencies (scenarios in which a single component fails). This research extends that benchmark in two directions, both directly inspired by my thesis results. The first is differential constraint violation: investigating whether different prediction targets — bus voltage, power flow, battery dispatch — violate their constraints at different perturbation levels, and whether this differential vulnerability is predictable from the model's internal representations. The second concerns the choice of predictive signal: in my thesis, I found that feature-variance collapse at a critical noise threshold predicted task failure before it occurred. The open question is what the equivalent internal signal might be in a GNN world model — candidates include the distribution of node-embedding norms, the magnitude of attention weights on physically critical edges, or the gradient of the constraint penalty with respect to the input perturbation. Identifying the most reliable predictor of imminent constraint violation has direct practical value: an operator who knows which constraint is about to be violated has time to intervene before the violation occurs.

## Expected Contributions

1. A theoretical analysis of which physical constraints governing three-phase power flow can be encoded as architectural priors in a heterogeneous GNN, and which require explicit penalty terms, together with formal characterizations of the expressivity and constraint-satisfaction guarantees of each approach.
2. A new architecture in which Kirchhoff's laws are encoded as inductive biases in the message-passing scheme, and operational constraints are enforced through a differentiable projection layer at the output.
3. A robustness evaluation protocol, implemented as an extension of the SafePowerGraph benchmark, that measures constraint satisfaction specifically under the structural perturbations — line outages, phase imbalances, extreme load events — that characterize safety-critical grid operation.
4. An empirical study of the relationship between physical law enforcement and out-of-distribution generalization, providing design guidelines for practitioners who need to deploy physics-informed world models on grid topologies not seen during training.

Beyond these technical contributions, this research aims to produce a conceptual contribution connecting two literatures that have not, to my knowledge, been formally linked: physics-informed machine learning, which asks how to embed physical laws into learned models, and physical reservoir computing, which asks what physical systems compute when governed by their own laws. My thesis provides a bridge — the conditions that make a physical reservoir robust to perturbation are the same conditions that physics-informed learning tries to engineer into a learned model. Formalizing this connection is a theoretical contribution that extends beyond power grids to any domain where the governing physical laws are known.

## Connection to the Supervisor's Research Programme

This proposal is designed as a direct extension of the group's existing research programme. The group's graph-embedding methodology for power flow prediction provides the foundation I will extend with physics-informed architectural priors. Its existing physics-aware constraint-enforcement framework for battery dispatch provides the approach I will generalize to the full AC power flow equations and evaluate under distribution shift. The SafePowerGraph benchmark provides the evaluation infrastructure I will extend with the structural perturbation protocol described above. I am not proposing a new research direction; I am proposing to close the gaps between existing directions the group has already established, and to connect them to a perspective — the physics-as-substrate perspective from physical reservoir computing — that the group has not yet had access to.
